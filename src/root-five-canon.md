# ROOT FIVE

## Credit, Debt & Future Income

### Core Question

**When I use money today that must be repaid tomorrow, what am I gaining, what am I promising, and what choices am I removing from my future?**

Root Five begins after the learner understands how money is earned, how it moves through present life, and how some of it can be carried forward for future protection.

The learner now enters the financial territory where future money can be brought into the present.

Borrowing can create access before enough money has been saved.

It can make housing, transportation, education, medical care, equipment, or emergency survival possible.

It can also increase the final cost of a purchase, restrict future cash flow, place property at risk, and create obligations that continue long after the original benefit has disappeared.

Root Five does not teach that debt is automatically irresponsible.

It does not divide borrowing into simplistic categories of “good debt” and “bad debt.”

It teaches the learner to examine the complete exchange:

* what becomes available now
* who is providing it
* what must be repaid
* when repayment is required
* what the full cost may become
* what future income is being committed
* what happens if circumstances change
* and what rights, responsibilities, and risks belong to each party

Borrowing is not free money.

It is a contract across time.

---

# PURPOSE OF ROOT FIVE

Many credit decisions are presented through one attractive number:

The payment.

A vehicle is advertised by the monthly payment.

A credit card emphasizes the minimum payment.

A checkout screen divides a purchase into four smaller amounts.

A mortgage advertisement displays an estimated principal-and-interest payment.

A student-loan offer focuses on access to enrollment.

But the payment is only one part of the obligation.

A complete evaluation may also require:

* the amount borrowed
* the interest rate
* the annual percentage rate
* the loan term
* fees
* collateral
* changing rates
* promotional expiration dates
* payment timing
* insurance requirements
* penalties
* total repayment
* and consequences of missed payments

Credit can expand present choice while reducing future flexibility.

Root Five teaches the learner to see both sides at the same time.

---

# ROOT FIVE LEARNING PROMISE

By the end of Root Five, the learner should be able to:

* explain how borrowing moves future purchasing power into the present
* identify the borrower, lender, principal, payment, term, and obligation
* distinguish access from affordability
* explain how debt claims future income
* recognize common borrowing costs
* distinguish interest rate from annual percentage rate
* understand fixed, variable, introductory, promotional, and penalty rates
* explain how repayment length affects payment size and total cost
* understand minimum payments
* compare cash price, financed price, and total repayment
* distinguish revolving and installment credit
* distinguish secured and unsecured debt
* recognize collateral risk
* understand common consumer-credit structures
* read the basic sections of a credit-card or loan statement
* explain the purpose of credit reports and credit scores
* distinguish current balance, statement balance, credit limit, and available credit
* understand credit utilization conceptually
* recognize hard and soft inquiries
* identify possible credit-report errors and fraud
* distinguish joint borrowers, cosigners, owners, and authorized users
* understand delinquency, default, charge-offs, and collections
* distinguish approval from affordability
* recognize debt spirals and unsustainable debt patterns
* create a complete debt inventory
* compare repayment approaches without being directed toward one
* examine consolidation, refinancing, and settlement tradeoffs
* map how much future income is already committed
* separate financial shame from the actual terms of the contract

## The Bridge District

Ivy and Eli leave the Reserve Lands and arrive at a wide canyon.

Across the canyon are places they recognize:

* homes
* schools
* hospitals
* vehicles
* stores
* repair stations
* workplaces
* and communities

The largest letters advertise:

“Only $299 per month.”

“Four easy payments.”

“Prequalified.”

“Minimum payment due.”

The smaller signs contain the complete terms.

Some bridges have fixed tolls.

Some tolls change.

Some require property to be pledged before entry.

Others merely make an expensive decision appear temporarily affordable.

Ivy sees opportunity.

Eli sees danger.

Sage stops them both.

“A bridge is not wise because it creates access,” she says. “And it is not automatically foolish because it carries a cost. We must examine where it leads, what it requires, and what happens if the traveler cannot finish crossing.”

# PART ONE

## UNDERSTANDING THE EXCHANGE

# LESSON ONE

## What Borrowing Actually Does

### Story

Sage takes Ivy and Eli to the first bridge.

At the entrance, Ivy receives $1,000.

At the exit, a contract requires her to return the $1,000 plus an additional cost over time.

Ivy says she has gained $1,000.

Sage corrects her.

She has gained temporary use of $1,000.

The money did not become income.

It arrived with an obligation attached.

Eli says borrowing should therefore always be avoided.

Sage corrects him too.

The borrowed money may allow someone to reach work, receive medical care, complete a credential, repair essential housing, or manage a temporary disruption.

The important question is not whether money was borrowed.

The question is what the borrower receives, what repayment requires, and whether the exchange remains workable over time.

### Financial Connection

Borrowing generally includes:

**Borrower:** The person or organization receiving money, goods, services, or access.

**Lender or creditor:** The person or organization providing the money or credit.

**Principal:** The original amount borrowed.

**Interest:** The cost charged for the use of the lender’s money.

**Payment:** The amount required at a particular time.

**Term:** The period allowed for repayment.

**Obligation:** The borrower’s contractual responsibility.

Borrowing does not create new income.

It changes timing by bringing future purchasing power into the present.

### Learner Reflection

Think of one time you or someone in your household borrowed money.

* What became possible immediately?
* What future payment was created?
* How long did the obligation continue?
* Did the benefit last as long as the repayment?

### Knowledge Check

Which statement is most accurate?

A. Borrowed money is additional income.
B. Borrowing moves future purchasing power into the present and creates a repayment obligation.
C. Borrowing always increases wealth.
D. Borrowing is always a financial failure.

**Correct answer: B**

### Story

### Financial Connection

Common borrowing costs may include:

**Prepayment penalty:** A possible charge for repaying certain loans early.

# LESSON FIVE

## Interest Rates and APR

### Story

Two bridge signs advertise different offers.

Offer A displays a lower interest rate.

Offer B displays a higher rate but fewer fees.

Ivy chooses the lower rate immediately.

Eli looks only at the monthly payment.

Sage directs them to the annual percentage rate.

She explains that rate information must be interpreted in context.

The interest rate describes one part of the cost.

APR may include certain additional borrowing costs, depending on the credit product.

Neither number should be read without the rest of the agreement.

### Financial Connection

**Interest rate:** The percentage charged on the borrowed balance.

**APR:** An annualized measure intended to help describe borrowing cost and, for some products, may include certain fees.

**Fixed rate:** A rate that remains unchanged under the agreement’s stated terms.

**Variable rate:** A rate that may change according to an index or contract provision.

**Introductory or promotional rate:** A temporary rate offered for a limited period.

**Penalty rate:** A higher rate that may apply after specified events.

The learner should ask:

* How long does the rate last?
* What causes it to change?
* What fees are included?
* What fees are excluded?
* What qualifications apply?

### Learner Reflection

Compare two fictional offers using:

* interest rate
* APR
* fees
* rate duration
* variable-rate terms
* total repayment

### Knowledge Check

Why might the lowest advertised interest rate not automatically create the lowest total cost?

**Correct understanding:** Fees, rate changes, term length, and other contract conditions may alter the total cost.

# LESSON SIX

## Time Changes the Cost

### Story

Ivy compares two repayment options.

The first has a lower monthly payment and a longer term.

The second has a higher payment and a shorter term.

Ivy prefers the lower payment because it fits more comfortably into the current month.

Eli prefers the shorter term because it carries less total interest.

Sage asks whether Eli’s preferred payment is sustainable in Ivy’s real cash flow.

It may not be.

The lower payment creates more immediate flexibility but may increase the total cost.

The higher payment may reduce total cost but create greater monthly risk.

### Financial Connection

A longer repayment term often:

* lowers the required monthly payment
* extends the obligation
* increases total interest
* may increase the risk of owing money after the item has lost significant value

A shorter term often:

* increases the required payment
* reduces the repayment period
* may reduce total interest
* may create less monthly flexibility

There is no universal best term without examining the learner’s circumstances.

### Learner Reflection

Compare a three-year and six-year fictional loan.

Ask:

* Which payment is easier to absorb?
* Which creates the higher total repayment?
* What changes if income falls?
* How long will the financed item remain useful?

### Knowledge Check

Can a lower monthly payment produce a higher total cost?

A. No
B. Yes
C. Only with credit cards
D. Only when a payment is late

**Correct answer: B**

* available credit

* grace period
* cash advance

# LESSON ELEVEN

## Secured and Unsecured Debt

### Story

Two bridges require different promises.

One requires Ivy to pledge a vehicle.

The other relies primarily on her contractual promise and creditworthiness.

The first is secured.

The second is unsecured.

The secured loan may offer different terms because the lender can claim the collateral after default, subject to the contract and applicable law.

### Financial Connection

**Secured debt:** Backed by collateral.

Examples may include:

* mortgages
* auto loans
* secured credit cards
* certain secured personal loans

**Unsecured debt:** Not directly backed by a specific pledged asset.

Examples may include:

* many credit cards
* medical debt
* many personal loans
* certain student loans

Unsecured does not mean consequence-free.

Collections, lawsuits, credit reporting, and other consequences may still occur.

### Learner Reflection

* whether collateral exists
* what property is at risk
* whether insurance is required
* what may happen after default

### Financial Connection

Credit should not be visually represented only as danger.

The bridges exist because they create access.

The complete cost becomes visible as the learner progresses.

Visual themes include:

* bridges
* toll gates
* contracts
* clocks
* payment calendars
* revolving loops
* installment stairways
* collateral vaults
* changing rate signs
* promotional banners
* minimum-payment tunnels
* credit archives
* approval checkpoints
* collection corridors
* future-income chains
* repayment exits
* returning pathways as debts end

### Visual progression

Early Root:

* bright bridge entrances
* large payment signs
* destinations visible across the canyon

Middle Root:

* smaller contract terms become visible
* tolls extend across time
* some bridges require collateral
* revolving paths loop back toward their beginnings

Later Root:

* future calendars fill with obligations
* collection routes and legal checkpoints appear
* repayment strategies create different paths
* completed obligations reopen portions of the landscape

Debt should not appear as chains attached to a bad person.

It should appear as a structural connection between present access and future income.

When an obligation ends, the visual reward should be the return of flexibility and choice.

# ROOT FIVE TONE

Sage should be:

* precise
* direct
* calm
* skeptical of incomplete advertising
* respectful of borrowing under pressure
* honest about cost
* careful about legal boundaries
* unwilling to confuse a credit score with character
* unwilling to confuse approval with affordability

Sage may say:

“The payment is visible. The complete obligation is not yet visible.”

“This borrowing created access. Now identify what future income it claims.”

“The rate is lower. We still need the term, fees, and total repayment.”

“The minimum payment may keep the account current without reducing it quickly.”

“You are describing shame. I am asking you to describe the contract.”

“The lender approved the risk. Your life still has to absorb the payment.”

“This account is charged off. That does not automatically tell us whether the obligation disappeared.”

“Before choosing a strategy, we need the complete inventory.”

“This option reduces one risk and may create another.”

Sage should not say:

“All debt is bad.”

“This is good debt.”

“Never use credit cards.”

“Always pay the smallest balance first.”

“Always pay the highest rate first.”

“You should consolidate.”

“You should settle.”

“You should file bankruptcy.”

“This lender is best.”

“A high credit score means you are financially successful.”

“If you borrowed for groceries, you were irresponsible.”

“Just stop using credit.”

# ROOT FIVE TRANSITION TO ROOT SIX

At the edge of the Bridge District, Ivy and Eli arrive at a protection checkpoint.

Behind them are the bridges that gave access while claiming future income.

Ahead are structures designed to protect:

* income
* property
* health
* identity
* family
* legal authority
* and accumulated assets

Some structures transfer risk.

Some reduce risk.

Some merely create the appearance of protection.

Sage asks:

**“You now understand what can claim your future income. But what protects your income, property, identity, health, family, and decisions when something goes wrong?”**

The learner enters Root Six with one central question:

# FINANCIAL PROTECTION & RISK

**What could threaten what I have built, and which tools reduce, transfer, contain, or prepare for that risk?**
